Day 1: Engineering for the Common Good
When Water Becomes a Matter of Survival 0-8 min

When a community faces a drought, water becomes more than a resource. It becomes a matter of survival. Civil engineers step in to design systems like aqueducts and bridges to move water from distant sources to those in need. To do this, they rely on the laws of physics. They use gravity to pull water downhill through pipes, carefully calculating the elevation change to ensure the water flows at the right speed. If the slope is too flat, the water stops. If it is too steep, the water can damage the system. Engineers also use tension to build strong bridges that support heavy water pipes across deep valleys.

For a Catholic engineer, these technical choices are also moral ones. In his letter Laudato Si', Pope Francis calls us to practice "care for our common home." This means engineering must be sustainable. We use the principle of Solidarity to remember that we are one human family, and the Option for the Poor to ensure that those with the least resources are served first.

Every project involves a ledger balance where we track our spending. Stewardship means balancing a budget while making ethical decisions. An engineer might choose more expensive materials if they are sourced sustainably or if the workers are paid a fair wage. While using cheaper materials might save money (a positive change for the budget), it might harm the environment or exploit workers (a negative impact on our common home). In engineering for the common good, we use math to ensure our designs are precise and our hearts to ensure they are just.
