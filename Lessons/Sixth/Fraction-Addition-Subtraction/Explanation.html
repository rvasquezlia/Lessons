The One Rule That Runs Everything: you can only add or subtract fractions when the pieces are the same size - the same denominator. If the denominators don't match, rename the fractions first.

Lesson Title: Fraction Addition & Subtraction

  • Like Denominators: add or subtract the numerators, keep the denominator, simplify.
  • Unlike Denominators: find the LCD, rename both fractions, then follow the like-denominator rule.
  • Mixed Numbers: combine the whole numbers and fraction parts separately - regroup if the fraction you're subtracting is bigger.
  • Whole Number + Fraction: adding just attaches the fraction; subtracting regroups the whole as a fraction first.

Bar Models: Why Denominators Must Match

Same-size sixths stack up directly: \(2 + 3 = 5\) sixths.

Estimation Strategy: Benchmark Fractions

Estimate first with a benchmark, then calculate exactly. If the exact answer isn't close to your estimate, something's off.

Common benchmarks: \(0\), \(\frac{1}{2}\), and \(1\).

Example: \(\frac{5}{9} + \frac{4}{7}\) - both fractions are close to \(\frac{1}{2}\), so the estimate is about \(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}=1\). The exact answer, \(\frac{71}{63} = 1\frac{8}{63}\), is very close to that estimate.

Rule #1: Like Denominators.

  1. Keep the denominator the same - it names the size of the pieces, and that doesn't change.
  2. Add or subtract only the numerators.
  3. Simplify the result to lowest terms.
  4. If the numerator ends up bigger than (or equal to) the denominator, convert the improper fraction to a mixed number.

Guided Word Problem: Step-by-Step

Scenario: Maria has \(\frac{7}{8}\) of a pizza left over. She eats \(\frac{3}{8}\) of the pizza for lunch. How much pizza does she have left?

Step 1: Check the denominators.
Both fractions are eighths - like denominators, so we can subtract right away.
Step 2: Subtract the numerators.
\[\frac{7}{8} - \frac{3}{8} = \frac{7-3}{8} = \frac{4}{8}\]
Step 3: Simplify.
\(\frac{4}{8}\) simplifies to \(\mathbf{\frac{1}{2}}\) - Maria has half a pizza left.

Rule #2: Unlike Denominators.

  1. Find the LCD (least common denominator) of the two fractions.
  2. Rename each fraction as an equivalent fraction with that denominator.
  3. Add or subtract the numerators, keeping the common denominator.
  4. Simplify the result, converting to a mixed number if it's improper.

Exercise 1 of 3

Extra Guided Example 1: Adding (\(\frac{2}{5} + \frac{1}{2}\))

Extra Guided Example 2: Subtracting (\(\frac{3}{4} - \frac{2}{5}\))

Guided Word Problem: Step-by-Step

Scenario: A recipe calls for \(\frac{2}{3}\) cup of flour for the cake and \(\frac{1}{4}\) cup of flour for the topping. How much flour is needed in total?

Step 1: Find the LCD of 3 and 4.
Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12. LCD = 12.
Step 2: Rename both fractions with denominator 12.
\[\frac{2}{3} = \frac{8}{12} \qquad \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{12}\]
Step 3: Add the numerators.
\[\frac{8}{12} + \frac{3}{12} = \frac{11}{12}\]
Step 4: Final Answer.
The recipe needs \(\mathbf{\frac{11}{12}}\) cup of flour in total. \(\frac{11}{12}\) is already in simplest form.

Rule #3: Mixed Numbers.

  1. Get common denominators for the fraction parts, if they're unlike.
  2. Add or subtract the fraction parts and the whole-number parts separately.
  3. Adding: if the fraction part is improper (\(\ge 1\)), regroup - carry 1 into the whole-number part.
  4. Subtracting: if the fraction you're subtracting is bigger than the fraction you have, regroup 1 whole from the whole-number part into an extra fraction first.
  5. Simplify the fraction part of your final answer.

Tip: regrouped whole numbers are shown in red in our examples so they're easy to spot on the page - that's just a classroom convention, not part of the math itself.

Guided Example Carousel: Step-by-Step

Exercise 1 of 3

Extra Guided Example 1: Adding With Regrouping (\(3\frac{2}{3} + 1\frac{5}{6}\))

Extra Guided Example 2: Subtracting With Borrowing (\(4\frac{1}{6} - 1\frac{1}{2}\))

Guided Word Problem: Step-by-Step

Scenario: A board is \(6\frac{1}{4}\) inches long. A carpenter cuts off a piece \(2\frac{3}{4}\) inches long. How much board is left?

Step 1: Denominators already match (fourths). Compare the fraction parts.
\(\frac{1}{4} < \frac{3}{4}\) - we can't subtract \(\frac{3}{4}\) from \(\frac{1}{4}\) without regrouping.
Step 2: Regroup 1 whole from 6 into \(\frac{4}{4}\).
\[6\frac{1}{4} = 5\frac{4}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = 5\frac{5}{4}\]
Step 3: Subtract whole numbers and fraction parts separately.
\[5\frac{5}{4} - 2\frac{3}{4} = (5-2)\frac{5-3}{4} = 3\frac{2}{4}\]
Step 4: Simplify.
\(3\frac{2}{4}\) simplifies to \(\mathbf{3\frac{1}{2}}\) inches of board left.

Rule #4: Whole Number + Fraction.

  1. Adding a fraction to a whole number just attaches it - a whole number is already "\(0\) parts short," so nothing to regroup. \(4 + \frac{2}{5} = 4\frac{2}{5}\).
  2. Subtracting a fraction from a whole number requires regrouping first - rewrite the whole number as (whole number \(- 1\)) plus a fraction equal to 1 with the needed denominator.
  3. Adding or subtracting a whole number and a mixed number just combines with the whole-number part - the fraction part doesn't change.

Guided Example Carousel: Step-by-Step

Exercise 1 of 3

Extra Guided Example: Subtracting a Fraction From a Whole Number (\(7 - \frac{3}{8}\))

Guided Word Problem: Step-by-Step

Scenario: Liam has 4 whole pizzas. He gives \(\frac{3}{4}\) of a pizza to a friend. How much pizza does he have left?

Step 1: Set up the problem.
\[4 - \frac{3}{4}\]
Step 2: Regroup 1 whole from 4 into fourths.
\[4 = 3\frac{4}{4}\]
Step 3: Subtract the fraction parts.
\[3\frac{4}{4} - \frac{3}{4} = 3\frac{1}{4}\]
Step 4: Final Answer.
Liam has \(\mathbf{3\frac{1}{4}}\) pizzas left.
