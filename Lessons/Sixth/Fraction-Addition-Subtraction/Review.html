Review: Fractions Before We Add & Subtract

Everything you need to remember before we start adding and subtracting fractions

Work through the tabs in order: vocabulary first, then equivalent fractions, then common denominators and mixed numbers, then a short practice check so you can see for yourself what's solid and what needs another look. Come back to any tab as often as you like, even after we've started the real lesson.
Why We're Reviewing This First
Adding and subtracting fractions builds directly on skills you already learned: naming a fraction, finding equivalent fractions, and converting between mixed numbers and improper fractions. This review isn't new material – it's making sure that foundation is solid, because every add/subtract-fractions problem you're about to learn depends on it. If finding a common denominator or converting a mixed number still feels shaky, that's what will trip you up – not the addition or subtraction itself.
The Big Idea

You can only add or subtract fractions when the pieces are the same size – that means the same denominator. Fifths can't combine with thirds any more than feet can combine with meters without converting first. Everything in this topic comes back to that one rule: get a common denominator, then add or subtract the numerators.

Vocabulary You Need
These words come up constantly, in this review and in the Fraction Addition & Subtraction lesson itself. Read through them once, and come back any time a word trips you up.
Numerator

The top number in a fraction – how many equal parts you have.

In \(\frac{3}{4}\), 3 is the numerator
Denominator

The bottom number in a fraction – how many equal parts the whole is split into.

In \(\frac{3}{4}\), 4 is the denominator
Equivalent Fractions

Fractions that name the same amount, even though the numbers look different.

\(\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{6}\)
Simplest Form

A fraction reduced as far as it can go – the numerator and denominator share no common factor except 1.

\(\frac{6}{8}\) simplifies to \(\frac{3}{4}\)
Multiple

The product of a number and any whole number.

Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20...
Least Common Multiple (LCM)

The smallest number that is a multiple of two or more numbers.

LCM of 4 and 6 is 12
Least Common Denominator (LCD)

The LCM of two fractions' denominators – the smallest denominator they can both convert into.

LCD of \(\frac{1}{4}\) and \(\frac{1}{6}\) is 12
Like & Unlike Denominators

Like denominators are the same; unlike denominators are different.

\(\frac{2}{5}\) and \(\frac{3}{5}\) are like; \(\frac{2}{5}\) and \(\frac{3}{4}\) are unlike
Mixed Number

A whole number and a fraction written together.

\(2\frac{1}{3}\)
Improper Fraction

A fraction whose numerator is greater than or equal to its denominator.

\(\frac{7}{3}\) is the same amount as \(2\frac{1}{3}\)
Benchmark Fraction

A familiar fraction (like \(\frac{1}{2}\), \(\frac{1}{4}\), or \(1\)) used to estimate or compare other fractions.

\(\frac{5}{9}\) is a little more than the benchmark \(\frac{1}{2}\)
Sum & Difference

The answer to an addition problem (sum) or a subtraction problem (difference).

\(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4}=\frac{2}{4}\): \(\frac{2}{4}\) is the sum
Equivalent Fractions & Simplest Form
Multiplying (or dividing) both the numerator and denominator by the same number renames a fraction without changing its value – you're just cutting the same amount into more (or fewer) pieces.
\(\frac{1}{2}\)
=
\(\frac{2}{4}\)
=
\(\frac{4}{8}\)
Building an Equivalent Fraction

\[\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1 \times 4}{2 \times 4} = \frac{4}{8}\]

Same rule works backward to simplify a fraction: divide both numbers by a common factor.

\[\frac{6}{8} = \frac{6 \div 2}{8 \div 2} = \frac{3}{4}\]

Simplest Form: How to Know You're Done

A fraction is in simplest form when the only number that divides evenly into both the numerator and denominator is 1. If you can still find a common factor, keep dividing.

Example: \(\frac{12}{18}\) → both divide by 6 → \(\frac{2}{3}\). Nothing but 1 divides evenly into both 2 and 3, so \(\frac{2}{3}\) is simplest form.

Finding a Common Denominator & Mixed Number Conversions
These are the two skills that make unlike-denominator and mixed-number problems possible. Both come back to multiples.
Finding the Least Common Denominator (LCD)
  1. List multiples of each denominator until you find one they share.
  2. The smallest shared multiple is the LCD.
  3. Convert each fraction to an equivalent fraction with that denominator.

Example: LCD of \(\frac{1}{4}\) and \(\frac{1}{6}\)?
Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16... Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18...
LCD = 12. So \(\frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{12}\) and \(\frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{12}\).

Mixed Number → Improper Fraction

Multiply the whole number by the denominator, add the numerator, and keep the same denominator.

\[2\frac{1}{3} \;\rightarrow\; \frac{(2 \times 3) + 1}{3} = \frac{7}{3}\]

Improper Fraction → Mixed Number

Divide the numerator by the denominator. The quotient is the whole number; the remainder becomes the new numerator over the same denominator.

\[\frac{11}{4} \;\rightarrow\; 11 \div 4 = 2 \text{ remainder } 3 \;\rightarrow\; 2\frac{3}{4}\]
