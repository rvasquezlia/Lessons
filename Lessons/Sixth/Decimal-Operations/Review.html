Review: Place Value & Whole-Number Operations

Everything you need to remember before we start Decimal Operations

Why We're Reviewing This First
Decimal Operations is the very first topic of the year, so this review isn't about "old" material – it's about making sure the foundation underneath it is solid. Every decimal algorithm you're about to learn (adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing decimals) is the exact same whole-number algorithm you already know, with one extra rule for where the decimal point goes. If lining up numbers, carrying, borrowing, or the multiplication and division steps still feel shaky, that is what will trip you up – not decimals themselves.
The Big Idea

A decimal number is just a way of writing a value that includes parts smaller than one whole, using the same base-ten place value system as whole numbers. If you understand what each digit in a whole number is worth, and you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide whole numbers confidently, you already have almost everything you need for decimals.

Vocabulary You Need
These words come up constantly, in this review and in the Decimal Operations lesson itself. Read through them once, and come back any time a word trips you up.
Digit

One of the ten symbols (0–9) used to build numbers. A number can be made of many digits.

In 4,829, the digits are 4, 8, 2, and 9
Place Value

The value a digit has because of its position in a number.

In 583, the 5 is worth 500 because it's in the hundreds place
Standard Form

A number written the normal way, with digits.

1,204
Expanded Form

A number written as the sum of the value of each digit.

1,204 = 1,000 + 200 + 0 + 4
Word Form

A number written out in words.

1,204 = one thousand, two hundred four
Decimal Point

The dot that separates the whole-number part of a number from the part that's less than one.

In 3.75, everything left of the dot is whole, everything right is a fraction of one
Regroup

Trading 10 of one place value for 1 of the next place value up (or the reverse). Also called carrying (addition) or borrowing (subtraction).

In 47 + 18, you regroup 10 ones into 1 ten
Sum / Difference

The answer to an addition problem (sum) or a subtraction problem (difference).

9 + 5 = 14: 14 is the sum. 9 - 5 = 4: 4 is the difference
Factor & Product

The numbers being multiplied are factors; the answer is the product.

6 × 7 = 42: 6 and 7 are factors, 42 is the product
Dividend, Divisor & Quotient

The number being split up is the dividend, the number you're dividing by is the divisor, and the answer is the quotient.

20 ÷ 4 = 5: 20 is the dividend, 4 is the divisor, 5 is the quotient
Remainder

Whatever is left over after division when the dividend doesn't split evenly.

17 ÷ 5 = 3 remainder 2
Round / Rounding

Replacing a number with a simpler, nearby number, based on a chosen place value.

36 rounds to 40 (nearest ten)

The Rule That Runs the Whole System

Moving one place to the left multiplies the value by 10. Moving one place to the right divides the value by 10. This rule doesn't stop at the ones place – it keeps going right through the decimal point, which is exactly why decimals work the same way whole numbers do.

Comparing Numbers

To compare two numbers, line up the place values (imagine the decimal points stacked on top of each other) and compare digit by digit, starting from the largest place value. The first place where the digits differ decides which number is bigger.

Example: compare 5.4 and 5.36. Line them up: \(5.40\) vs. \(5.36\). Ones match (5 = 5). Tenths: 4 > 3. So \(5.4 > 5.36\) – even though 5.36 "looks longer," it's actually smaller.

Whole-Number Operations: The Same Steps You'll Use With Decimals
Every algorithm below is one you've used before. Step through each one and make sure you can still explain why each step works, not just how to do it – that's what makes the decimal version easy later.
Addition: Line Up & Carry

Line up digits by place value (ones under ones, tens under tens...). Add each column from right to left. If a column's sum is 10 or more, write the ones digit and carry the rest to the next column.

  367
+ 258
625

7 + 8 = 15 → write 5, carry 1. 6 + 5 + 1(carried) = 12 → write 2, carry 1. 3 + 2 + 1(carried) = 6.

Subtraction: Line Up & Borrow

Line up digits the same way. Subtract each column from right to left. If the top digit is smaller than the bottom digit, borrow 1 from the column to the left (which is worth 10 in the current column).

  513
- 176
337

3 − 6 needs a borrow: 13 − 6 = 7 (the 1 becomes 0). 0 − 7 needs a borrow: 10 − 7 = 3 (the 5 becomes 4). 4 − 1 = 3.

Multiplication: Multiply, Then Add Partial Products

Multiply the top number by each digit of the bottom number (right to left), shifting each new row one place left. Add all the partial products together.

Example: \(24 \times 13\). \(24 \times 3 = 72\). \(24 \times 10 = 240\). \(72 + 240 = \mathbf{312}\).

Division: The Long Division Steps

Divide, Multiply, Subtract, Bring down – repeat. This same four-step rhythm is exactly what you'll use once a decimal point gets involved.

Example: \(84 \div 4\). \(8 \div 4 = 2\); \(2 \times 4 = 8\); \(8 - 8 = 0\); bring down the 4. \(4 \div 4 = 1\). Quotient: \(\mathbf{21}\).
