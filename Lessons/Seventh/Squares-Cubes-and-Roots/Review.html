Why We're Learning This
You already know how to multiply a number by itself – that's a square. Multiplying a number by itself three times gives you a cube. This topic teaches you to work backward too: given the result, find the number that produced it. That "working backward" operation is called a root, and it's the doorway to the next big idea in math – numbers that aren't rational at all.
Building on Rational Numbers

You've spent this unit computing confidently with rational numbers – fractions and decimals, positive and negative. Squares and cubes reuse that same multiplication fluency, just applied to a number times itself. Square and cube roots reverse the process.

Vocabulary You Need First
Square (\(n^2\))

A number multiplied by itself one time.

\(6^2 = 6 \times 6 = 36\)
Cube (\(n^3\))

A number multiplied by itself, then by itself again (three factors total).

\(4^3 = 4 \times 4 \times 4 = 64\)
Perfect Square

A number that is the square of a whole number.

36 is a perfect square, since \(6^2 = 36\)
Perfect Cube

A number that is the cube of a whole number.

64 is a perfect cube, since \(4^3 = 64\)
Square Root (\(\sqrt{n}\))

The "undo" of squaring: what number times itself equals \(n\)?

\(\sqrt{36} = 6\), since \(6^2 = 36\)
Cube Root (\(\sqrt[3]{n}\))

The "undo" of cubing: what number, used as a factor three times, equals \(n\)?

\(\sqrt[3]{64} = 4\), since \(4^3 = 64\)
Radical

The root symbol itself (\(\sqrt{\phantom{x}}\) or \(\sqrt[3]{\phantom{x}}\)), and any expression that uses it.

\(\sqrt{49}\) is a radical expression
Base

The number being multiplied by itself in a square or cube.

In \(7^2\), the base is 7
