Converting Between Decimals & Fractions
Fractions and decimals are two ways of writing the same number. You'll need to move between them constantly on this test, so both directions have to be automatic.
The Two Conversions
To convert...Do this
Fraction → decimalDivide the numerator by the denominator (long division).
Decimal → fractionWrite the digits over the matching place value - tenths → \(/10\), hundredths → \(/100\), thousandths → \(/1000\) - then simplify.
Mixed number → decimalConvert only the fraction part, then add it to the whole number: \(8\dfrac{1}{4} = 8 + 0.25 = 8.25\).
Rational or Irrational? Divide It Out
You only need one tool to answer every classification question on the test: do the division, and look at what happens to the decimal.
The Rule

If the decimal terminates (the division stops, remainder becomes 0) or repeats (a block of digits comes back forever), the number is rational. If it goes on forever with no repeating pattern - like \(\pi = 3.14159265\ldots\) - it's irrational. Since this class doesn't use square roots, the only irrational numbers you'll see are non-repeating decimals like \(\pi\). Any number that starts as a fraction of two integers can only ever terminate or repeat when you divide it out, so it is always rational.

Example 1: Convert a Fraction to a Decimal - \(\dfrac{5}{12}\) Conversion
  1. 1To convert a fraction to a decimal, divide the numerator by the denominator.
    \(5 \div 12\)
  2. 212 goes into 50 four times (\(4 \times 12 = 48\)), leaving a remainder of 2. Bring down a zero to make 20.
    \(50 \div 12 = 4\), remainder \(2\)
  3. 312 goes into 20 once (\(1 \times 12 = 12\)), leaving a remainder of 8. Bring down a zero to make 80.
    \(20 \div 12 = 1\), remainder \(8\)
  4. 412 goes into 80 six times (\(6 \times 12 = 72\)), leaving a remainder of 8 again - the same remainder as before, so from here the digit 6 repeats forever.
    \(80 \div 12 = 6\), remainder \(8\) (repeats)
\(\dfrac{5}{12} = 0.41\overline{6}\) - a repeating decimal
Example 2: Convert a Decimal to a Fraction - \(0.65\) Conversion
  1. 1\(0.65\) has two decimal places, so it belongs over the hundredths place value: denominator 100.
    \(0.65 = \dfrac{65}{100}\)
  2. 2Simplify by dividing the numerator and denominator by their GCF, 5.
    \(\dfrac{65 \div 5}{100 \div 5} = \dfrac{13}{20}\)
\(0.65 = \dfrac{13}{20}\)
Example 3: Classify a Repeating Decimal - Is \(0.41\overline{6}\) rational or irrational? Classify
  1. 1A number is rational whenever it can be written as a fraction of two integers - and any decimal that terminates or repeats can always be rewritten that way.
  2. 2\(0.41\overline{6}\) is repeating (the 6 repeats forever), so it fits that description - it's actually the very same number as \(\dfrac{5}{12}\) from Example 1.
Rational
Example 4: Classify a Non-Repeating Decimal - Is \(\pi = 3.14159265\ldots\) rational or irrational? Classify
  1. 1Look at the decimal digits of \(\pi\): they never end, and no matter how far you calculate, they never settle into a repeating block.
  2. 2Since \(\pi\) cannot be written as an exact fraction of two integers, it does not fit the definition of a rational number.
Irrational
How to Add or Subtract Signed Numbers
One process, three checks, every time - for integers, decimals, and fractions alike.
Same-Sign / Different-Sign Method
CheckWhat to do
Is it addition or subtraction?Addition → go straight to the next check. Subtraction → Keep-Change-Change first: keep the first number, change the operation to addition, change the second number's sign to its opposite. Now it's addition.
Are the signs the same or different?Compare the sign of the two numbers now being added.
What do I do with the numbers, and what sign does the answer get?Same signs: add the absolute values, keep the common sign. Different signs: subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger, and keep the sign of whichever number has the greater absolute value.
Butterfly Method: Adding & Subtracting Fractions
  1. If it's subtraction, rewrite it as addition first (Keep-Change-Change).
  2. Cross-multiply each numerator by the other fraction's denominator - these are the two "wings."
  3. Multiply the two denominators straight down the middle for the new denominator.
  4. Apply the Same-Sign / Different-Sign Method to the two wing values - they're just two signed numbers now.
  5. Simplify the result to lowest terms.
Example 1: Adding Integers, Same Sign - \(-16 + (-9)\) Addition
  1. 1Both numbers are negative, so the signs are the same - add the absolute values and keep the common sign.
    \(16 + 9 = 25\)
\(-16 + (-9) = -25\)
Example 2: Subtracting Decimals - \(-9.3 - (-4.7)\) Subtraction
  1. 1This is subtraction, so rewrite it as addition first using Keep-Change-Change: keep \(-9.3\), change subtraction to addition, and change \(-4.7\) to \(+4.7\).
    \(-9.3 - (-4.7) = -9.3 + 4.7\)
  2. 2Now it's addition with different signs, so subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger.
    \(9.3 - 4.7 = 4.6\)
  3. 3The number with the greater absolute value is \(-9.3\), so the answer is negative.
\(-9.3 + 4.7 = -4.6\)
Example 3: Adding Signed Fractions (Butterfly Method) - \(-\dfrac{3}{5} + \left(-\dfrac{1}{10}\right)\) Addition
  1. 1Cross-multiply each numerator by the other fraction's denominator to get the two wings.
    \(3 \times 10 = 30\) and \(5 \times 1 = 5\)
  2. 2Multiply the denominators straight down the middle for the new denominator.
    \(5 \times 10 = 50 \;\Rightarrow\; -\dfrac{30}{50} + \left(-\dfrac{5}{50}\right)\)
  3. 3Same signs, so add the wings and keep the common sign.
    \(30 + 5 = 35 \;\Rightarrow\; -\dfrac{35}{50}\)
  4. 4Simplify by dividing by the GCF, 5.
\(-\dfrac{3}{5} + \left(-\dfrac{1}{10}\right) = -\dfrac{7}{10}\)
Example 4: Adding Decimals, Different Signs - \(8.4 + (-13.1)\) Addition
  1. 1This is already addition, with different signs - subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger.
    \(13.1 - 8.4 = 4.7\)
  2. 2The number with the greater absolute value is \(-13.1\), so the answer is negative.
\(8.4 + (-13.1) = -4.7\)
Quick special case - subtracting a negative from zero: \(0 - (-13.25)\) rewrites (Keep-Change-Change) as \(0 + 13.25\), and adding zero never changes a number. So \(0 - (-13.25) = 13.25\). Any time you see "minus a negative," it becomes simple addition.
Fractions or Decimals? Choosing a Form
When one number is a fraction and the other is a decimal, you can't add or subtract them yet - convert them to the same form first.
Deciding Which Way to Convert
  • Convert the fraction to a decimal when its denominator is "friendly" (only 2s and/or 5s: 2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 20, 25, 50, 100...) - it will divide out evenly.
  • Convert the decimal to a fraction when the decimal repeats, or when the rest of the problem is already in fractions.
  • Not sure which is easier? Either direction works - just pick one and stay consistent for the whole problem.
Multi-Step Strategy

Work two numbers at a time. Solve the first pair completely with the Same-Sign / Different-Sign Method, then treat that result as the new "first number" and repeat with the next one - the same process, just climbed more than once.

Example 1: Adding a Fraction and a Decimal - \(-\dfrac{5}{8} + 0.75\) Mixed Forms
  1. 1One number is a fraction and one is a decimal, so convert to match. \(-\dfrac{5}{8}\) has a denominator of 8 (only 2s) - a "friendly" denominator - so convert it to a decimal.
    \(-\dfrac{5}{8} = -0.625\)
  2. 2Now both numbers are decimals with different signs - subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger.
    \(0.75 - 0.625 = 0.125\)
  3. 3The number with the greater absolute value is \(0.75\), so the answer is positive.
\(-\dfrac{5}{8} + 0.75 = 0.125\)
Example 2: Three Terms, Multi-Step - \(15.6 - 8\dfrac{1}{4} + (-1.6)\) Multi-Step
  1. 1Convert the mixed number to a decimal first.
    \(8\dfrac{1}{4} = 8 + 0.25 = 8.25\)
  2. 2Work the first two numbers: \(15.6 - 8.25\) is subtraction with different signs, so subtract. The answer is positive, since \(15.6\) has the greater absolute value.
    \(15.6 - 8.25 = 7.35\)
  3. 3Now combine that result with the last number: \(7.35 + (-1.6)\) has different signs, so subtract. The answer is positive, since \(7.35\) has the greater absolute value.
    \(7.35 - 1.6 = 5.75\)
\(15.6 - 8\dfrac{1}{4} + (-1.6) = 5.75\)
Word Problem 1: Temperature - At sunrise it was \(-6.25\)°C. By noon it rose \(9\dfrac{1}{2}\)°C. During an evening storm it dropped \(4.8\)°C. What was the final temperature? Word Problem
  1. 1Translate the words into an expression: start at \(-6.25\); "rose \(9\dfrac{1}{2}\)" means add \(9.5\); "dropped \(4.8\)" means subtract \(4.8\).
    \(-6.25 + 9.5 - 4.8\)
  2. 2Combine the first two numbers: different signs, so subtract. Positive, since \(9.5\) has the greater absolute value.
    \(-6.25 + 9.5 = 3.25\)
  3. 3Combine with the last number: \(3.25 - 4.8\) rewrites as \(3.25 + (-4.8)\), different signs, so subtract. Negative, since \(-4.8\) has the greater absolute value.
    \(4.8 - 3.25 = 1.55\)
Final temperature: \(-1.55\)°C
Word Problem 2: Depth - A submarine at \(-18.6\) m descends another \(12\dfrac{3}{4}\) m, then ascends \(9.35\) m. What is its final depth? Word Problem
  1. 1Translate: start at \(-18.6\); "descends another \(12\dfrac{3}{4}\)" means add \(-12.75\) (descending makes it more negative); "ascends \(9.35\)" means add \(+9.35\).
    \(-18.6 + (-12.75) + 9.35\)
  2. 2Combine the first two numbers: same signs (both negative), so add and keep the common sign.
    \(18.6 + 12.75 = 31.35 \;\Rightarrow\; -31.35\)
  3. 3Combine with the last number: \(-31.35 + 9.35\) has different signs, so subtract. Negative, since \(-31.35\) has the greater absolute value.
    \(31.35 - 9.35 = 22.0\)
Final depth: \(-22\) m
