Converting Between Decimals & Fractions
Fractions and decimals are two ways of writing the same number. You'll need to move between them constantly on this test, so both directions have to be automatic.
The Two Conversions
To convert...Do this
Fraction → DecimalDivide the numerator by the denominator (long division).
Decimal → FractionWrite the digits over the matching place value - tenths → \(/10\), hundredths → \(/100\), thousandths → \(/1000\) - then simplify.
Mixed number → DecimalConvert only the fraction part, then add it to the whole number: \(8\dfrac{1}{4} = 8 + 0.25 = 8.25\).
Rational or Irrational? Divide It Out
You only need one tool to answer every classification question related to Rational Numbers: do the division, and look at what happens to the decimal.
The Rule

If the decimal terminates (the division stops, remainder becomes 0) or repeats (a block of digits comes back forever), the number is rational. If it goes on forever with no repeating pattern - like \(\pi = 3.14159265\ldots\) - it's irrational. The only not rational numbers you'll see are non-repeating decimals like \(\pi\).

Example 1: Convert a Fraction to a Decimal - \(\dfrac{5}{12}\) Conversion

To convert a fraction to a decimal, divide the numerator by the denominator - set it up as the same long division you used in 6th grade, adding zeros after the decimal point as needed.

\[ \begin{array}{r} 0.4166 \\ 12\overline{)5.0000} \\ 50\phantom{000} \\ -48\phantom{000} \\ \hline 20\phantom{00} \\ -12\phantom{00} \\ \hline 80\phantom{0} \\ -72\phantom{0} \\ \hline 80 \\ -72 \\ \hline 8 \end{array} \]

Bring down a zero and keep dividing by 12 at each step. Watch the remainders on the right: 8 shows up twice in a row, which means the division will never reach a remainder of 0 - it just keeps repeating the same steps forever. Since the remainder repeated, the quotient digit that produced it (6) repeats forever too, so instead of writing 6s forever we mark just that one with a bar on top: \(0.4166\ldots = 0.41\overline{6}\).

\(\dfrac{5}{12} = 0.41\overline{6}\) - a repeating decimal
Example 2: Convert a Decimal to a Fraction - \(0.65\) Conversion
  1. 1\(0.65\) has two decimal places, so it belongs over the hundredths place value: denominator 100.
    \(0.65 = \dfrac{65}{100}\)
  2. 2Simplify by dividing the numerator and denominator by dividing by 5.
    \(\dfrac{65 \div 5}{100 \div 5} = \dfrac{13}{20}\)
\(0.65 = \dfrac{13}{20}\)
Example 3: Classify a Repeating Decimal - Is \(0.41\overline{6}\) rational or irrational? Classify
  1. 1A number is rational whenever it can be written as a fraction of two integers - and any decimal that terminates or repeats can always be rewritten that way.
  2. 2\(0.41\overline{6}\) is repeating (the 6 repeats forever), so it fits that description - it's actually the very same number as \(\dfrac{5}{12}\) from Example 1.
Rational
Example 4: Classify a Non-Repeating Decimal - Is \(\pi = 3.14159265\ldots\) rational or irrational? Classify
  1. 1Look at the decimal digits of \(\pi\): they never end, and no matter how far you calculate, they never settle into a repeating block.
  2. 2Since \(\pi\) cannot be written as an exact fraction of two integers, it does not fit the definition of a rational number.
Irrational
How to Add or Subtract Signed Numbers
One process, three checks, every time - for integers, decimals, and fractions alike. Ask yourself the same questions in the same order, no matter what the numbers look like.
Same-Sign / Different-Sign Method - Ask Yourself These Questions
Ask...Then...
Is it addition or subtraction?Addition → go straight to the next check. Subtraction → Keep-Change-Change first: keep the first number, change the operation to addition, change the second number's sign to its opposite. Now it's addition.
Are the signs the same or different?Compare the sign of the two numbers now being added.
What do I do with the numbers, and what sign does the answer get?Same signs: add the absolute values, keep the common sign. Different signs: subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger, and keep the sign of whichever number has the greater absolute value.
Butterfly Method: Adding & Subtracting Fractions
  1. If it's subtraction, rewrite it as addition first (Keep-Change-Change).
  2. Cross-multiply each numerator by the other fraction's denominator - these are the two "wings."
  3. Multiply the two denominators straight down the middle for the new denominator.
  4. Apply the Same-Sign / Different-Sign Method to the two wing values - they're just two signed numbers now.
  5. Simplify the result to lowest terms.

Example 1: Adding Integers, Same Sign - \(-16 + (-9)\) Addition
\(-16 + (-9)\)
Is this addition or subtraction?
Addition already addition - nothing to rewrite.
Do the two numbers have the same sign or different signs?
Same - both numbers are negative.
What do I do with the numbers?
Add the absolute values.
Resolving\(16 + 9 = 25\)
Which sign does the answer keep?
The common sign - negative.
\(-16 + (-9) = -25\)
Example 2: Subtracting Decimals - \(-9.3 - (-4.7)\) Subtraction
\(-9.3 - (-4.7)\)
Is this addition or subtraction?
Subtraction - apply Keep-Change-Change before doing anything else.
Keep-Change-Change
Keep \(-9.3\) Change \(-\) to \(+\) Change \(-4.7\) to \(+4.7\)
\(-9.3 - (-4.7) \;\rightarrow\; -9.3 + 4.7\)
Now: same signs or different signs?
Different
What do I do with the numbers?
Subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger.
Resolving - difference of the absolute values (not the final answer yet)\(9.3 - 4.7 = 4.6\)
Which number has the greater absolute value?
\(-9.3\), so the answer is negative.
\(-9.3 - (-4.7) = -4.6\)
Example 3: Adding Signed Fractions (Butterfly Method) - \(-\dfrac{3}{5} + \left(-\dfrac{1}{10}\right)\) Addition
\(-\dfrac{3}{5} + \left(-\dfrac{1}{10}\right)\)
Is this addition or subtraction?
Addition already addition - nothing to rewrite.
Do the fractions have the same denominator?
No - build a common one with the Butterfly Method first.
Building the wings\(3 \times 10 = 30\) and \(5 \times 1 = 5\); denominators straight down the middle: \(5 \times 10 = 50\) \(\;\Rightarrow\; -\dfrac{30}{50} + \left(-\dfrac{5}{50}\right)\)
Do the wing values have the same sign or different signs?
Same - both negative.
What do I do with the numbers?
Add the wing values.
Resolving\(30 + 5 = 35 \;\Rightarrow\; -\dfrac{35}{50}\)
Which sign does the answer keep?
The common sign - negative. Then simplify by the GCF, 5.
\(-\dfrac{3}{5} + \left(-\dfrac{1}{10}\right) = -\dfrac{7}{10}\)
Example 4: Adding Decimals, Different Signs - \(8.4 + (-13.1)\) Addition
\(8.4 + (-13.1)\)
Is this addition or subtraction?
Addition already addition - nothing to rewrite.
Same signs or different signs?
Different
What do I do with the numbers?
Subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger.
Resolving - difference of the absolute values\(13.1 - 8.4 = 4.7\)
Which number has the greater absolute value?
\(-13.1\), so the answer is negative.
\(8.4 + (-13.1) = -4.7\)
Quick special case - subtracting a negative from zero: \(0 - (-13.25)\) rewrites (Keep-Change-Change) as \(0 + 13.25\), and adding zero never changes a number. So \(0 - (-13.25) = 13.25\). Any time you see "minus a negative," it becomes simple addition.
Fractions or Decimals? Choosing a Form
When one number is a fraction and the other is a decimal, you can't add or subtract them yet - convert them to the same form first.
Deciding Which Way to Convert
  • Convert the fraction to a decimal when its denominator is "friendly" (only 2s and/or 5s: 2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 20, 25, 50, 100...) - it will divide out evenly.
  • Convert the decimal to a fraction when the decimal repeats, or when the rest of the problem is already in fractions.
  • Not sure which is easier? Either direction works - just pick one and stay consistent for the whole problem.
Multi-Step Strategy

Work two numbers at a time. Solve the first pair completely with the Same-Sign / Different-Sign Method, then treat that result as the new "first number" and repeat with the next one - the same process, just climbed more than once.
