Topic Overview: Understanding Rational Numbers
Fractions & Decimals - a bridge and review topic.
  • Sits right after Integers. Does not teach negative-number operations - that's the separate Operations with Rational Numbers topic.
  • Students pin down what a rational number is, convert fluently between fractions and decimals, and plot/compare on a number line.
  • Fraction and decimal arithmetic is a refresher here, not first-time instruction.
  • Pages don't link to each other - share each one directly (a Google Classroom post per page works well) in the order you want your class to work through it.

Standards & Objectives

CCSS.Math.Content.7.NS.A.2d (convert a rational number to a decimal using long division; know that repeating decimals eventually repeat) and foundational review of 6.NS.A / 5.NBT.B / 5.NF.A-B fraction and decimal operations.

  • Define a rational number and identify where rational and irrational numbers sit in the number system.
  • Convert fractions to decimals (recognizing terminating vs. repeating, and bar notation) and decimals to fractions.
  • Plot rational numbers on a number line and compare/order them, including a few negative examples.
  • Sharpen previously-learned fraction operations (unlike denominators, multiplication, division by reciprocal) and decimal operations (add, subtract, multiply, divide).
This Is a Bridge Topic - Framing Matters

Tabs 5-6 of Explanation, and the operation-heavy tabs of Practice Set and Word Problems, are review, not new instruction - students already learned fraction/decimal arithmetic. Say so out loud: "you already have this toolkit - we're sharpening it before we use it on negative rational numbers next topic."

Materials Needed
  • One device per student for all six pages - no printed materials needed. The Timed Review Quiz and Mixed Practice both run on-screen from Test Prep.
Common Misconceptions to Watch For
  • Adding or subtracting fractions straight across (numerator + numerator, denominator + denominator) instead of finding a common denominator.
  • Treating a repeating decimal as an approximation of a fraction rather than an exact, equal value.
  • Misaligning decimal points when adding or subtracting, or forgetting to count decimal places when multiplying.
  • Dividing fractions by multiplying straight across instead of flipping the second fraction (Keep-Change-Flip).
  • Comparing two negative rational numbers backwards - forgetting that the value closer to zero is greater.
Differentiation Notes

Not required daily assignments - use for targeted reinforcement, extension, or support based on observed skill gaps.

  • Support: students who struggle with the LCD for fraction addition/subtraction benefit from a multiplication-table reference and from writing out multiples of each denominator side by side before choosing the LCD.
  • Support: for long division producing a repeating decimal, let students stop after 3-4 decimal digits and describe the pattern in words before introducing bar notation formally.
  • Extension: challenge early finishers to find a fraction between any two given rational numbers, or to convert a repeating decimal like \(0.\overline{45}\) back to a fraction by inspection (\(\frac{45}{99} = \frac{5}{11}\)) as a preview of the algebraic technique they'll formalize later.
Suggested Metacognition Prompts
  • "How do you decide whether it's easier to compare two rational numbers as fractions or as decimals?"
  • "What's one fraction or decimal operation from earlier grades that came back to you quickly, and one that needed more review?"
  • "Why does a repeating decimal represent an exact value rather than just a very close approximation?"

3. Explanation

Fully worked examples only, nothing graded. Tabs 1-4 teach this topic's actual new content (classifying rational numbers, converting both directions between fractions and decimals, comparing/ordering); Tabs 5-6 are explicitly a review of fraction and decimal arithmetic from earlier grades, not new instruction - say that out loud when you assign them, so students don't feel like they're re-learning something for the first time.

TabWhat it's about
1. What Is a Rational Number?Defines a rational number as any \(\frac{a}{b}\) with integers \(a,b\) and \(b \neq 0\), and places it in the natural/whole/integer/rational/irrational hierarchy, worked through a 3-example carousel.
2. Fractions to DecimalsThe long-division rule, plus the terminate-vs-repeat test (a denominator whose only prime factors are 2 and/or 5 terminates; anything else repeats) - this is the rule underneath the whole unit's terminating-vs-repeating misconception.
3. Decimals to FractionsWriting the digits over the matching place value (tenths→10, hundredths→100, thousandths→1000), then simplifying with the GCF.
4. Compare & OrderConvert both values to the same form (decimals are fastest) then plot on a number line - the exact strategy Practice Set's own Compare & Order tab expects students to already have.
5. Fraction Operations (Review)The Butterfly Method for add/subtract, straight-across for multiply, Keep-Change-Flip for divide - Grade 5-6 review, not new instruction.
6. Decimal Operations (Review)Aligning decimal points before add/subtract, counting decimal places for multiply/divide - same review status as Tab 5.

The main graded computation page - pure numbers, no word problems, across 6 tabs your class can work in any order. Every item allows 2 tries with a reveal, so a student who doesn't recover still sees the correct process.

Metacognition & Discussion

Reflection prompts and small-group discussion questions to use alongside any of the six pages - none of this is graded content on a student page, it's for you to drop into a warm-up, an exit ticket, or a mid-lesson pause.

Reflection Prompts (use as an exit ticket or journal stem)
  • "When I convert a fraction to a decimal, how can I tell in advance whether it will terminate or repeat - before I even start dividing?"
  • "If my division gives me a repeating decimal like 0.1666..., how do I decide which digit(s) go under the bar, and why is writing it as 0.17 instead of \(0.1\overline{6}\) not just 'close enough'?"
  • "Where did I use the sign rules (or Keep-Change-Change) today, and did I check the sign of my final answer before moving on?"
  • "Which mistake would I catch faster in someone else's work than in my own - adding fractions straight across, or misaligning decimal points? Why do you think that is?"
  • "When I compared two rational numbers today, did I convert them to the same form first - and how did I decide whether fractions or decimals would be faster?"
Leveled Small-Group Discussion Prompts
TierPrompt
Building UnderstandingAs a group, take turns converting \(\frac{3}{8}\) to a decimal by long division. Why does this one terminate while \(\frac{1}{3}\) doesn't? What's different about the two denominators?
ApplyingSolve \(-\frac{5}{6} - \frac{1}{4}\) and \(5.6 + (-3.75)\) as a group, splitting the work between fraction and decimal problems. Where did the sign rules from Integers show up in each one, and did everyone land on the same sign for both answers?
ExtendingDesign a real-world example where comparing a fraction and a decimal actually matters (who ran faster, which recipe uses more sugar) - one where getting the comparison backward would lead to the wrong real-world conclusion. Trade with another group and see if they agree with your reasoning.

Scaffolded Support Problems

For a student still guessing rather than reasoning through conversion or the sign rules. Read each hint aloud before they attempt the problem - unlike the graded pages, the scaffolding is meant to be visible here, not discovered.

ProblemHint to giveAnswer
1. Convert \(\frac{5}{8}\) to a decimal.Divide the numerator by the denominator - 5 ÷ 8. Check the denominator's factors first: 8 is only 2's, so this one should terminate.\(\mathbf{0.625}\)
2. Convert \(\frac{1}{6}\) to a decimal.This denominator has a factor of 3, so watch for a repeating pattern as you divide - don't stop after two or three digits without checking if it's repeating.\(\mathbf{0.1\overline{6}}\) (accept 0.167)
3. Evaluate \(-\frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{2}\).Find a common denominator first, then use the sign rule for different signs: subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger and keep the sign of the number with the larger absolute value.\(\mathbf{-\frac{1}{4}}\)
4. Evaluate \(-\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{5}\).Multiply straight across, then simplify. Different signs in a multiplication always give a negative result - decide the sign before you even multiply.\(\mathbf{-\frac{2}{5}}\)

Stays within this unit's own scope (converting, comparing, and the four operations on rational numbers - the standards on this file's own standards line) while pushing past what's graded on any of the six pages.

ProblemAnswer / Notes
1. Evaluate \(-\frac{5}{6} + \frac{1}{3} \times \left(-\frac{3}{4}\right)\).Multiply first: \(\frac{1}{3} \times \left(-\frac{3}{4}\right) = -\frac{1}{4}\). Then \(-\frac{5}{6} + \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) = -\frac{10}{12} - \frac{3}{12} = \mathbf{-\frac{13}{12}}\) (or \(-1\frac{1}{12}\)) - combines order of operations with the multiply and add rules from this unit.
2. Convert \(0.\overline{18}\) to a fraction, using the pattern that a repeating block goes over that many 9's.The repeating block "18" is 2 digits, so it goes over 99: \(\frac{18}{99} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{11}}\) - a preview by inspection of the algebraic technique students formalize in a later grade.
3. Construct two rational numbers - one written as a fraction, one as a decimal - whose difference is exactly \(-\frac{1}{2}\). Extra problems for early finishers or homework extension at any point in this topic. Each is a step above Practice Set/Word Problems difficulty. Click a problem to reveal its full solution.

Number System & Conversion - Harder Tier

C1. Convert \(\frac{7}{11}\) to a decimal. Identify the repeating block.

Show solution

\(7 \div 11 = 0.636363\ldots = \mathbf{0.\overline{63}}\) - the block "63" repeats.

C2. Order from least to greatest: \(0.7,\ \frac{5}{7},\ 0.72,\ \frac{13}{18}\).

Show solution

As decimals: \(0.7,\ 0.714\ldots,\ 0.72,\ 0.722\ldots\). Order: \(\mathbf{0.7,\ \frac{5}{7},\ 0.72,\ \frac{13}{18}}\).

C3. Which is larger, \(\frac{9}{13}\) or \(0.69\)?

Show solution

\(\frac{9}{13} = 0.6923\ldots\), which is greater than \(0.69\). \(\mathbf{\frac{9}{13}}\) is larger.

C4. Convert \(0.0625\) to a fraction in simplest form.

Show solution

\(0.0625 = \frac{625}{10000}\). Divide both by their GCF, 625: \(\mathbf{\frac{1}{16}}\).

C5. Is every integer a rational number? Explain using the definition \(\frac{a}{b}\).

Show solution

Yes. Any integer \(n\) can be written as \(\frac{n}{1}\), which fits the definition of a rational number with \(b = 1 \neq 0\).

Fraction & Decimal Operations - Harder Tier

C1. Evaluate \(\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{4}\).

Show solution

LCD = 12: \(\frac{8}{12} + \frac{2}{12} - \frac{3}{12} = \mathbf{\frac{7}{12}}\).

C2. Evaluate \(3.6 \times 0.25\).

Show solution

\(36 \times 25 = 900\); 3 total decimal places: \(\mathbf{0.9}\).

C3. Evaluate \(\frac{5}{8} \div \frac{15}{16}\).

Show solution

\(\frac{5}{8} \times \frac{16}{15} = \frac{80}{120} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}}\).

C4. Evaluate \(\left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}\right) \times 6\).

Show solution

\(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3} = \frac{5}{6}\); \(\frac{5}{6} \times 6 = \mathbf{5}\).

C5. Evaluate \(4.5 \div 1.25\).

Show solution

Move both decimal points 2 places: \(450 \div 125 = \mathbf{3.6}\).

Error Analysis - Harder Tier

C1. A student computed \(\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{8}\). Find the mistake and the correct answer.

Show solution

The student multiplied straight across instead of flipping the second fraction. Correct: \(\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{2}{1} = \mathbf{\frac{3}{2}}\).

C2. A student computed \(0.7 - 0.35 = 0.45\). Find the mistake and the correct answer.

Show solution

The student subtracted digits without borrowing correctly. Correct: \(0.70 - 0.35 = \mathbf{0.35}\).

C3. A student computed \(\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}\). Find the mistake and the correct answer.

Show solution

The student added denominators too, then "simplified" incorrectly. Since the denominators already match, only add the numerators: \(\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{3} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}}\).

C4. A student computed \(2.5 \times 0.1 = 2.5\). Find the mistake and the correct answer.

Show solution

The student forgot to shift the decimal point at all. Correct: \(25 \times 1 = 25\), with 2 total decimal places: \(\mathbf{0.25}\).

C5. A student says \(\frac{5}{6}\) is bigger than \(\frac{7}{8}\) "because 6 is less than 8." Find the mistake and the correct comparison.

Show solution

You can't compare fractions by their denominators alone. \(\frac{5}{6} = 0.8\overline{3}\) and \(\frac{7}{8} = 0.875\), so \(\mathbf{\frac{7}{8}}\) is actually bigger.

Real-World Precision - Harder Tier

C1. A tank holds 12.5 gallons. If \(\frac{2}{5}\) of the tank is full, how many gallons are in the tank?

Show solution

\(12.5 \times \frac{2}{5} = \mathbf{5}\) gallons.

C2. A rope 8.4 m long is cut into pieces 0.6 m each. How many pieces?

Show solution

\(8.4 \div 0.6 = \mathbf{14}\) pieces.

C3. A recipe needs \(\frac{3}{4}\) cup sugar and yields 6 cookies. How much sugar per cookie?

Show solution

\(\frac{3}{4} \div 6 = \mathbf{\frac{1}{8}}\) cup per cookie.

C4. Find the average of \(0.75\), \(\frac{5}{8}\), and \(0.8\).

Show solution

As decimals: \(0.75 + 0.625 + 0.8 = 2.175\); \(2.175 \div 3 = 0.725 = \mathbf{\frac{29}{40}}\).

C5. A car's gas tank is \(\frac{3}{8}\) full and holds 16 gallons total. How many more gallons are needed to fill it completely?

Show solution

Empty fraction: \(1 - \frac{3}{8} = \frac{5}{8}\). \(\frac{5}{8} \times 16 = \mathbf{10}\) gallons.

Enrichment for Early Finishers

C1. Find a fraction between \(\frac{1}{4}\) and \(\frac{1}{3}\).

Show solution

Rewrite with denominator 24: \(\frac{1}{4} = \frac{6}{24}\), \(\frac{1}{3} = \frac{8}{24}\). Any fraction between them works, e.g. \(\mathbf{\frac{7}{24}}\).

C2. Write three different fractions equivalent to \(\frac{2}{5}\).

Show solution

Any fraction that simplifies to \(\frac{2}{5}\), e.g. \(\mathbf{\frac{4}{10},\ \frac{6}{15},\ \frac{8}{20}}\).

C3. A decimal repeats in blocks of 2 and equals \(\frac{4}{33}\). Find the decimal.

Show solution

\(4 \div 33 = 0.121212\ldots = \mathbf{0.\overline{12}}\).

C4. If \(0.1\overline{6} = \frac{1}{6}\), what is \(0.8\overline{3}\) as a fraction?

Show solution

\(0.8\overline{3} = 0.8333\ldots = \mathbf{\frac{5}{6}}\) (the same value worked out in Explanation, Tab 2).

C5. True or False: between any two rational numbers, there is always another rational number. Explain.

Show solution

True. Their average is always rational and always falls strictly between them - which means there are infinitely many rational numbers between any two you pick.
