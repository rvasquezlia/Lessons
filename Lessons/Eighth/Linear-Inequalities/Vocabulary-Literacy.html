Key Vocabulary: Solving Linear Inequalities
Every term below shows up in this topic. Read through them once, then come back any time a word trips you up.

Parts of an Inequality

Inequality

A mathematical sentence that compares two expressions using \(<\), \(>\), \(\leq\), or \(\geq\), showing they are not necessarily equal.

Example: \(x + 3 > 10\)
Solution of an Inequality

A value that makes the inequality a true statement when substituted for the variable.

\(x = 8\) is a solution of \(x > 5\), since \(8 > 5\)
Solution Set

The complete collection of every value that makes an inequality true - usually infinitely many values, not just one.

"All values of \(x\) greater than 5"
Boundary Point

The number that separates the values that make the inequality true from the values that don't.

In \(x > 5\), the boundary point is \(5\)
Isolate the Variable

The goal of solving: use inverse operations until the variable stands alone on one side - the same goal as solving an equation.

\(3x > 15 \rightarrow x > 5\)
Properties of Inequality

Rules that let you add, subtract, multiply, or divide both sides of an inequality by the same amount - except multiplying or dividing by a negative number, which flips the direction of the inequality.

If \(a < b\) and \(c\) is negative, then \(ac > bc\)

Graphing an Inequality

Open Circle

Used when graphing \(<\) or \(>\) - shows the boundary point itself is not a solution.

Graphing \(x > 3\) uses an open circle at \(3\)
Closed Circle

Also called a filled circle. Used when graphing \(\leq\) or \(\geq\) - shows the boundary point itself is a solution.

Graphing \(x \leq -2\) uses a closed circle at \(-2\)
Direction of the Arrow

The arrow on a number-line graph points toward every number that makes the inequality true - right for "greater than," left for "less than."

\(x > 3\) shades/arrows to the right of \(3\)

Real-World Phrases

At Least

Means "greater than or equal to" - the minimum amount is itself allowed.

"At least 5" means \(x \geq 5\)
At Most

Means "less than or equal to" - the maximum amount is itself allowed.

"At most 5" means \(x \leq 5\)
More Than

Means "strictly greater than" - the boundary value itself does not count.

"More than 5" means \(x > 5\)
Fewer Than / Less Than

Means "strictly less than" - the boundary value itself does not count.

"Fewer than 5" means \(x < 5\)
