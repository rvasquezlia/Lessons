Inequality Symbols & Graphing Basics
An inequality compares two expressions instead of setting them equal. Every inequality has a solution set - usually infinitely many values - which is why we graph it on a number line instead of naming just one number.
SymbolMeaningReal-World PhraseCircle
\(>\)Greater thanMore than, exceedsOpen
\(<\)Less thanFewer than, belowOpen
\(\geq\)Greater than or equal toAt least, no less thanClosed (filled)
\(\leq\)Less than or equal toAt most, no more thanClosed (filled)
The Two Questions Every Graph Answers

1. What kind of circle? Open (\(<\) or \(>\)) means the boundary point itself is not a solution. Closed/filled (\(\leq\) or \(\geq\)) means it is a solution.
2. Which direction? "Greater than" points right (toward bigger numbers); "less than" points left (toward smaller numbers).

Worked Example: Graph \(x > 3\)

\(>\) is strict, so \(3\) is not included → open circle at \(3\). "Greater than" points right.

Worked Example: Graph \(x \leq -2\)

\(\leq\) includes the boundary → closed (filled) circle at \(-2\). "Less than" points left.

Worked Example: Graph \(x \geq -6\)

\(\geq\) includes the boundary → closed circle at \(-6\). "Greater than" points right.

Reading a Graph Backwards

Given a graph, work the two questions in reverse: an open circle means a strict symbol (\(<\) or \(>\)); a filled circle means \(\leq\) or \(\geq\). An arrow pointing left means "less than"; pointing right means "greater than." An open circle at \(-1\) with an arrow to the left graphs \(x < -1\).

One-Step and Two-Step Inequalities
Solving an inequality uses the exact same inverse operations as solving an equation - undo addition/subtraction, then undo multiplication/division. As long as you only add, subtract, multiply, or divide by a positive number, the direction of the inequality symbol never changes.
Worked Example: One-Step (Addition/Subtraction)

Solve \(x + 5 > 12\). Subtract \(5\) from both sides: \(x > 7\).

Worked Example: One-Step (Subtraction)

Solve \(x - 6 \leq 3\). Add \(6\) to both sides: \(x \leq 9\).

Worked Example: One-Step (Positive Division)

Solve \(3x < 21\). Divide both sides by \(3\) - a positive number, so the symbol stays the same: \(x < 7\).

Worked Example: Two-Step

Solve \(3x - 4 < 11\).

Step 1 - Add 4 to both sides: \(3x < 15\).

Step 2 - Divide both sides by 3 (positive): \(x < 5\).

Worked Example: Two-Step

Solve \(2x + 7 \geq 15\).

Step 1 - Subtract 7 from both sides: \(2x \geq 8\).

Step 2 - Divide both sides by 2 (positive): \(x \geq 4\).

The Flip Rule: Multiplying or Dividing by a Negative
This is the one rule that makes inequalities different from equations. Whenever you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality by a negative number, you must flip the direction of the inequality symbol. Forgetting this single step is the most common mistake in this whole topic.
Why Does the Symbol Have to Flip?

Start with a true statement: \(4 < 9\). Multiply both sides by \(-1\) without changing the symbol: \(-4 < -9\). Is that true? No - \(-4\) is actually greater than \(-9\) on the number line. To keep the statement true, the symbol has to flip: \(-4 > -9\). Multiplying by a negative number reverses which side is "bigger," so the inequality symbol has to reverse too.

Worked Example: Dividing by a Negative

Solve \(-2x > 8\).

Divide both sides by \(-2\). Because \(-2\) is negative, flip the symbol: \(x < -4\).

Check it: try \(x = -5\) (should work): \(-2(-5) = 10 > 8\). True! Try \(x = -3\) (should not work, since it's not less than \(-4\)): \(-2(-3) = 6 > 8\)? False - correctly excluded.

Worked Example: Multiplying by a Negative

Solve \(-x \leq 5\).

Multiply both sides by \(-1\) to make the variable positive. Because \(-1\) is negative, flip the symbol: \(x \geq -5\).

Worked Example: Two Steps, Flip on the Last One

Solve \(-3x + 4 > 19\).

Step 1 - Subtract 4 from both sides: \(-3x > 15\).

Step 2 - Divide by \(-3\) (flip!): \(x < -5\).

The Mistake to Avoid

The flip only happens when you multiply or divide by a negative. Adding or subtracting a negative number never flips the symbol. In \(-3x + 4 > 19\) above, subtracting \(4\) doesn't flip anything - only the division by \(-3\) does.

Multi-Step Inequalities
Multi-step inequalities combine everything so far: distribute, combine like terms, collect the variable on one side, and isolate it - watching on every step for a multiplication or division by a negative.
Worked Example: Variables on Both Sides (No Flip Needed)

Solve \(2(x + 3) < 4x - 2\).

Step 1 - Distribute: \(2x + 6 < 4x - 2\).

Step 2 - Subtract \(2x\) from both sides: \(6 < 2x - 2\).

Step 3 - Add 2 to both sides: \(8 < 2x\).

Step 4 - Divide by 2 (positive): \(4 < x\), which is the same as \(x > 4\).

Step-by-Step: Distribute, Combine, and Flip

Problem: \(-3(x - 2) + 5 \leq 2x + 17\)

Worked Applications: Real-World Inequalities
Word problems that use "at least," "at most," "more than," or "fewer than" are always inequalities, not equations - there's a limit or requirement, not one exact amount.
Worked Example: A Budget Limit ("At Most")

A school trip costs \(\$45\) per student, plus a flat \(\$150\) bus fee. The total budget is at most \(\$600\). How many students, \(n\), can go?

Write it: "at most" means \(\leq\). \(45n + 150 \leq 600\).

Solve: subtract 150: \(45n \leq 450\). Divide by 45 (positive): \(n \leq 10\).

Interpret: at most \(\mathbf{10}\) students can go.

Worked Example: A Minimum Requirement ("At Least")

You need at least 20 points to pass a class contest. You already have 12 points, and each additional assignment is worth 4 points. How many more assignments, \(x\), do you need?

Write it: "at least" means \(\geq\). \(12 + 4x \geq 20\).

Solve: subtract 12: \(4x \geq 8\). Divide by 4 (positive): \(x \geq 2\).

Interpret: you need at least \(\mathbf{2}\) more assignments.

The 4-Step Method for Word Problems
  • Define: what does the variable represent, and what are its units?
  • Write: find the limit/requirement phrase and translate it into an inequality.
  • Solve: isolate the variable, watching for the flip rule.
  • Interpret: answer the actual question in a full sentence, with units.
