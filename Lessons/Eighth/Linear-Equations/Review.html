Review: Expressions, Like Terms & the Distributive Property

Everything you need to remember before we start Solving Linear Equations

How this page works Nothing here is graded – this is a warm-up, and it's an important one. Almost every mistake in solving equations traces back to simplifying – combining terms that shouldn't be combined, distributing incorrectly, or a sign error carried over from integer operations. Before you ever isolate a variable, you have to be able to simplify both sides of an equation without a mistake. Work through every tab here, even if it feels like review of review – this is the single most important prerequisite for the whole topic.
Why We're Reviewing This First
Solving a linear equation always happens in two phases: first simplify, then isolate. The "isolate" phase (inverse operations, properties of equality) is brand new and is what the Explanation page teaches. The "simplify" phase – combining like terms and distributing – is not new. It's a skill from previous grades that this topic assumes you already have. If you can't simplify \(3(2x+5)-4x\) into \(2x + 15\) confidently, the equation-solving itself will feel much harder than it actually is, because you'll be learning two skills at once instead of one.
The Big Idea

An expression is like a sentence written in math. Just like you can't add "3 apples" and "2 oranges" and call the answer "5 apple-oranges," you can't combine every pair of terms in an expression – only ones that match. Learning to tell which terms match (and which only look like they should) is most of what this page covers.

What This Page Covers
  • Like Terms: identifying them, combining them, and – just as important – recognizing what can never be combined.
  • The Distributive Property: multiplying a number into parentheses correctly, including with negative numbers.
  • Integer Rules Recap: the sign rules from 7th grade, since every simplification step depends on them.
  • A short practice check so you know exactly what to brush up on before we begin.
Vocabulary Quick Reference
A fast reference for the words used on this page. For the full glossary (including equation-solving vocabulary like solution and inverse operations) plus translation practice, see this topic's own Vocabulary & Literacy page.
Variable

A letter that represents an unknown or changing number.

The \(x\) in \(4x - 3\)
Expression

A combination of numbers, variables, and operations – with no equal sign.

\(4x - 3\)
Term

A single number, variable, or product/quotient of numbers and variables, separated from other terms by \(+\) or \(-\).

In \(4x + 7\), the terms are \(4x\) and \(7\)
Coefficient

The number multiplied by a variable in a term.

In \(6x\), the coefficient is \(6\)
Constant

A term that is just a fixed number, with no variable attached.

In \(3x + 9\), \(9\) is the constant
Like Terms

Terms with the exact same variable part (same letter, same exponent), so they can be combined.

\(5x\) and \(-2x\) are like terms
Like Terms: What Can (and Cannot) Combine
Two terms are "like terms" only if their variable part matches exactly – same letter(s), same exponent(s). The coefficient (the number out front) never matters for this decision. If the variable part doesn't match exactly, the terms can never be combined, no matter how similar they look.
How to Combine Like Terms

Add or subtract the coefficients only, and keep the variable part exactly as it was. Think of it like counting objects: \(5x\) means "5 x's" and \(-2x\) means "negative 2 x's," so \(5x + (-2x) = 3x\) – "5 x's minus 2 x's leaves 3 x's."

Yes, These Combine
Combine\(5x\) and \(-2x\)Both are just "\(x\)" – same variable, same exponent (1)
Combine\(7\) and \(-4\)Both are plain numbers (constants)
Combine\(3x^2\) and \(9x^2\)Both are "\(x\) squared" – same variable, same exponent (2)
Combine\(-6xy\) and \(2xy\)Both have exactly the variable part "\(xy\)"
No, These Do NOT Combine – And Here's Why
Cannot Combine\(5x\) and \(5x^2\)Different exponents (1 vs. 2) – \(x\) and \(x^2\) are different quantities
Cannot Combine\(4x\) and \(4y\)Different letters – \(x\) and \(y\) represent different unknowns
Cannot Combine\(3\) and \(3x\)One is a constant, one has a variable – only combine matching variable parts
Cannot Combine\(2xy\) and \(2x\)\(xy\) and \(x\) are different variable parts, even though both contain "\(x\)"

The takeaway: matching coefficients never matters, and a shared letter is never enough on its own. Only an exact match of the whole variable part (letters and exponents together) allows two terms to combine.

Worked Example: Combining Like Terms in a Longer Expression

Simplify \(7x + 3 - 2x + 9 - x\).

Step 1 – Sort the terms by type: \(x\)-terms: \(7x, -2x, -x\). Constants: \(3, 9\).

Step 2 – Combine each group: \(7x - 2x - x = 4x\). \(3 + 9 = 12\).

Result: \(4x + 12\).

The Distributive Property
When a number sits right outside a set of parentheses, it multiplies every single term inside – not just the first one. \[a(b + c) = ab + ac\]
Worked Example: Positive Multiplier

Simplify \(3(2x + 5)\). Multiply the 3 into both terms: \(3 \times 2x = 6x\), and \(3 \times 5 = 15\). Result: \(6x + 15\).

Worked Example: Negative Multiplier (Watch the Signs!)

Simplify \(-4(x - 6)\). This is the step students lose the most points on – the negative sign has to distribute too.

\(-4 \times x = -4x\), and \(-4 \times (-6) = +24\) (different signs become negative, same signs become positive – here both are negative, so the result is positive). Result: \(-4x + 24\).

Worked Example: Distribute, Then Combine Like Terms

Simplify \(3(2x + 5) - 4x\) – the exact expression from the "Why Review This" tab.

Step 1 – Distribute: \(3(2x+5) = 6x + 15\), so the expression becomes \(6x + 15 - 4x\).

Step 2 – Combine like terms: \(6x - 4x = 2x\).

Result: \(2x + 15\).

Integer Rules Recap
Every simplification above depends on adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing signed numbers correctly. This is the same table from Rational Numbers and Operations with Rational Numbers – still true, still essential.
The Rules
OperationThe Rule
AdditionSame signs: add the absolute values and keep the sign. (\(-3+(-5)=-8\))
Different signs: subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger, and keep the sign of the number with the larger absolute value. (\(-10+4=-6\))
SubtractionAdd the opposite: flip the subtraction to addition and change the sign of the second number. (\(5-(-2)\) becomes \(5+2=7\))
Multiplication & DivisionSame signs: the result is positive. Different signs: the result is negative.
