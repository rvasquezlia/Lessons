Topic Overview: Literal Equations
Literal equations are equations and formulas with more than one variable - \(A=lw\), \(d=rt\), \(F=\frac95C+32\), \(I=Prt\). The skill is solving for one specified variable in terms of the others, treating every other letter exactly as you'd treat a number. This is the single biggest conceptual hurdle in the topic: many students expect a numeric answer and freeze when the "answer" is an expression full of letters. Every page in this topic is built to confront that misconception head-on rather than paper over it with drill.
One Method, Used Everywhere: The Question Ladder

Every page asks the same four questions each step: variable? block? operation? inverse? Variable in >1 term → factor first.

Topic Objectives
  • Recognize a literal equation as an equation with more than one variable, and connect solving it to the numeric equations students already know.
  • Solve a literal equation for a specified variable when that variable appears exactly once.
  • Solve a literal equation for a specified variable when doing so requires an addition/subtraction step before the final division.
  • Solve a literal equation for a specified variable that appears more than once, by factoring it out.
  • Apply literal equations to real geometry, science, and finance formulas, and verify a rearrangement by substituting numbers into both forms.
Standards & Objectives

HSA.CED.A.4 (rearrange formulas to solve for a quantity of interest), HSA.REI.A.1 (explain each solving step) & MP.1, MP.2, MP.7 (look for and make use of structure).

Multidisciplinary connections: Geometry (perimeter, area, surface area, volume formulas); Physical Science (temperature conversion, Ohm's Law, kinematics); Personal Finance (simple interest); Mathematical Modeling (slope-intercept form, arithmetic sequences).

Common Misconceptions to Watch For
  • Expecting a numeric answer instead of an expression - "I can't solve this, there's no number" is the single most common thing you'll hear. The Explanation page's opening tab is built specifically to head this off.
  • Trying to combine letters that aren't like terms, such as adding \(m\) and \(b\) in \(y=mx+b\).
  • Attempting to isolate a variable that appears more than once without factoring first - see the Explanation page's Tab 4 and the Practice Set's Error Analysis tab.
  • Dropping a needed pair of parentheses in the final answer (writing \(\frac{P-2l}2\) as \(P-\frac{2l}2\) or similar), which silently changes the value.
  • Sign errors when the target variable's coefficient is negative or when dividing a difference by a negative number.

What each of the seven pages covers, and what each tab inside it is for. Answer keys for every graded item already live inline on the student page itself - sign in there as a teacher and it unlocks automatically (see the howto note above), so this tab is about what to teach, not what to grade.

1. Review

The pre-requisite refresher, not new content - it revisits exactly the two skills every literal-equation rearrangement depends on (the inverse-operations solving process, and the habit of moving an unfamiliar term exactly the way you'd move a number) before the topic's own vocabulary and formulas ever appear. Assign it first if a class is shaky on numeric equation-solving; a class that's already solid there can skip straight to Vocabulary & Literacy.

Metacognition & Discussion

Reflection prompts and small-group discussion questions to use alongside any of the seven pages - none of this is graded content on a student page, it's for you to drop into a warm-up, an exit ticket, or a mid-lesson pause.

Reflection Prompts (use as an exit ticket or journal stem)
  • "Today, did I ever divide (or multiply) only part of one side instead of every term on it? How did I catch that, or how would I catch it next time?"
  • "If I dropped the parentheses when writing \(\dfrac{P-2l}{2}\) as \(P-\dfrac{2l}{2}\), what would actually change about the value - and how would I explain that to a friend who thinks it's the same thing?"
  • "Which formula today made me lose track of which letter I was actually solving for? What helped me remember?"
  • "Where did I need to factor a variable out of two terms today (like \(P\) in \(A=P+Prt\)), and how did I know to do that instead of trying to divide right away?"
  • "On a scale of 1-5, how confident am I checking a rearranged formula by substituting numbers back into the original? What's one thing that would move me up a point?"
Leveled Small-Group Discussion Prompts
TierPrompt
Building UnderstandingTake turns explaining to your partner: why does every letter you're not solving for get treated exactly like a number? What would go wrong if you tried to "solve" \(b\) out of \(y=mx+b\) the same way you'd solve for \(x\)?
ApplyingAs a group, solve \(S=2lw+2lh+2wh\) for \(h\) two different ways if you can (e.g. one person groups the \(h\)-terms first, another moves the non-\(h\) term first before factoring). Do you land on the same final expression? Where could each path have gone wrong?
ExtendingDesign your own three-variable formula where your assigned target variable appears in two different terms, the way \(P\) does in \(A=P+Prt\). Trade with another group - can they identify the factoring step on their own, without you telling them to?

Scaffolded Support Problems

For a student who is still guessing rather than reasoning through the Question Ladder. Read each hint aloud before they attempt the problem - unlike the graded pages, the scaffolding is meant to be visible here, not discovered.

ProblemHint to giveAnswer
1. \(d=rt\), solve for \(r\)What's the target letter attached to right now? What's it doing to \(r\)? Undo that with the opposite operation.\(\mathbf{r=\dfrac{d}{t}}\)
2. \(P=2l+2w\), solve for \(w\)Two things are attached to \(w\): \(2l\) is being added, and \(2\) is multiplying the whole \(w\)-term. Undo the addition first, then the multiplication.\(\mathbf{w=\dfrac{P-2l}{2}}\)
3. \(F=\dfrac95C+32\), solve for \(C\)Subtract 32 first. Then, since \(\dfrac95\) is multiplying \(C\), multiply both sides by its reciprocal, \(\dfrac59\), instead of trying to divide by a fraction.\(\mathbf{C=\dfrac59(F-32)}\)
4. \(A=P+Prt\), solve for \(P\)\(P\) shows up in two separate terms - can one inverse operation remove both at once? No, so factor \(P\) out of the right side first, then divide.\(\mathbf{P=\dfrac{A}{1+rt}}\)

Goes beyond what's graded on any of the seven pages while staying strictly within this unit's own standard (HSA.CED.A.4 - rearranging a formula/literal equation for a specified variable) - no numeric linear-equation solving and no inequality notation, both of which belong to sibling units.

ProblemAnswer / Notes
1. The ideal gas law is \(PV=nRT\). Solve for \(R\).\(\mathbf{R=\dfrac{PV}{nT}}\) - only one inverse operation is needed, but this is a genuinely new formula (four letters, none of them used anywhere else in this unit) rather than a re-skin of a scenario students have already seen.
2. Solve \(mx+n=px-q\) for \(x\).\(\mathbf{x=\dfrac{q+n}{p-m}}\) - the target variable appears on both sides, which never happens on any of the graded pages in this unit (every graded literal equation here has the target on one side only). Collect the \(x\)-terms on one side first, then factor and divide, watching every sign carefully.
3. The area of a trapezoid is \(A=\dfrac12h(b_1+b_2)\). Solve for \(b_2\).\(\mathbf{b_2=\dfrac{2A}{h}-b_1}\) - a new formula with subscripted variables, which forces a student to be precise about exactly which letter is the target instead of leaning on a formula they've already memorized.

Explanation-Tier Challenges

C1. Ohm's Law: \(V = IR\). Solve for \(R\).

Show solution

\(R\) is multiplied by \(I\). Divide both sides by \(I\): \(R = \mathbf{\dfrac{V}{I}}\). Check: if \(V=120\), \(I=8\), then \(R=15\) and \(8 \times 15 = 120\).

C2. Solve \(2(x+y) = z\) for \(y\).

Show solution

Distribute: \(2x+2y=z\). Subtract \(2x\): \(2y=z-2x\). Divide by 2: \(y = \mathbf{\dfrac{z-2x}{2}}\). Check: if \(x=3, z=20\), then \(y=7\) and \(2(3+7)=20\).

C3. The average of two numbers is \(m = \dfrac{a+b}{2}\). Solve for \(b\).

Show solution

Multiply both sides by 2: \(2m = a+b\). Subtract \(a\): \(b = \mathbf{2m-a}\). Check: if \(a=5, m=12\), then \(b=19\) and \(\dfrac{5+19}{2}=12\).

Practice-Set-Tier Challenges

C1. Solve \(3x+2y=12\) for \(y\).

Show solution

Subtract \(3x\): \(2y=12-3x\). Divide by 2: \(y = \mathbf{\dfrac{12-3x}{2}}\). Check: if \(x=2\), \(y=3\) and \(3(2)+2(3)=12\).

C2. Kinetic energy: \(KE = \frac12mv^2\). Solve for \(m\).

Show solution

Multiply both sides by 2: \(2KE = mv^2\). Divide by \(v^2\): \(m = \mathbf{\dfrac{2KE}{v^2}}\). Check: if \(KE=90, v=6\), then \(m=5\) and \(\frac12(5)(36)=90\).

C3. Solve \(ax-b=cx+d\) for \(x\) (the target variable is on both sides).

Show solution

Collect the \(x\)-terms: subtract \(cx\) from both sides and add \(b\) to both sides: \(ax-cx=d+b\). Factor \(x\) out: \(x(a-c)=b+d\). Divide by \((a-c)\): \(x = \mathbf{\dfrac{b+d}{a-c}}\). Check: if \(a=5,c=2,b=3,d=9\), then \(x=4\), and \(5(4)-3=17=2(4)+9\).

Word-Problems-Tier Challenges

C1. The average of three test scores is \(m=\dfrac{a+b+c}{3}\). Two scores are 82 and 91, and the average is 88. What was the third score?

Show solution

Solve for \(c\): \(c=3m-a-b\). \(c = 3(88)-82-91 = 264-173 = \mathbf{91}\). Check: \((82+91+91)/3=88\).

C2. Ohm's Law: \(V=IR\). A circuit has a voltage of 240 V and a resistance of 60 Ω. What is the current?

Show solution

\(I = V/R = 240/60 = \mathbf{4}\) amps.

C3. A ball's velocity while decelerating under gravity follows \(v=v_0-gt\), with \(g=9.8\) m/s\(^2\). If the ball's velocity is \(0\) m/s after \(2\) seconds, what was its initial velocity \(v_0\)?

Show solution

Solve for \(v_0\): \(v_0=v+gt\). \(v_0 = 0+9.8(2) = \mathbf{19.6}\) m/s.

Test-Prep Enrichment for Early Finishers

These are original problems, unrelated to the graded assessment, safe to hand out to students who finish early.

C1. Solve \(5x+3y=2x-y+20\) for \(y\).

Show solution

Collect y-terms: add \(y\) to both sides and subtract \(2x\) from both sides: \(3x+4y=20\) becomes... more directly: \(3y+y=2x-5x+20 \rightarrow 4y=-3x+20\). Divide by 4: \(y = \mathbf{\dfrac{20-3x}{4}}\). Check: if \(x=4\), \(y=2\), and \(5(4)+3(2)=26=2(4)-2+20\).

C2. The thin-lens formula is \(\dfrac1a+\dfrac1b=\dfrac1f\). Solve for \(a\).

Show solution

Subtract \(\frac1b\) from both sides: \(\dfrac1a = \dfrac1f-\dfrac1b = \dfrac{b-f}{fb}\). Take the reciprocal of both sides: \(a = \mathbf{\dfrac{fb}{b-f}}\). Check: if \(f=6, b=12\), then \(a=12\), and \(\frac1{12}+\frac1{12}=\frac16\).

C3. Two pipes filling a tank together follow \(\dfrac{x}{a}+\dfrac{x}{b}=1\), where \(x\) is the time to fill it together. Solve for \(x\).

Show solution

Multiply every term by \(ab\) to clear denominators: \(bx+ax=ab\). Factor \(x\) out: \(x(a+b)=ab\). Divide by \((a+b)\): \(x = \mathbf{\dfrac{ab}{a+b}}\). Check: if \(a=3,b=6\), then \(x=2\), and \(\frac23+\frac13=1\).
