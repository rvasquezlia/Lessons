Review: Solving Equations, Recapped

Everything you need to remember before we start Literal Equations

How this page works Nothing here is graded – this is a warm-up. Literal Equations doesn't teach a new way to solve an equation – it takes the exact method from Solving Linear Equations and applies it to formulas with more than one letter. If you can solve \(2x + 5 = 17\) confidently, you're most of the way to solving \(P = 2l + 2w\) for \(l\). Work through the tabs in order.
Why We're Reviewing This First
Every technique in Literal Equations is borrowed directly from Solving Linear Equations: inverse operations, the properties of equality, combining like terms, distributing. The only genuinely new idea is that some of the "numbers" floating around in the equation are actually other letters – and those letters just get treated the same way a number would be. If the numeric solving process isn't automatic yet, that's the gap this page closes.
The Big Idea

Solving any equation is the same four-word process, every time: simplify, then isolate. Simplify each side first (distribute, combine like terms). Then use inverse operations, in reverse order, to peel away everything that isn't the variable you want – performing the same operation on both sides every time. That process doesn't change one bit in Literal Equations.

Vocabulary Quick Reference
A fast reminder of the equation-solving vocabulary from last topic. (Words like literal equation, formula, and subject of the formula are brand new this topic, so they're taught in this topic's own Vocabulary & Literacy page, not here.)
Properties of Equality

Rules that let you add, subtract, multiply, or divide both sides of an equation by the same amount without changing its solution.

If \(a = b\), then \(a + c = b + c\)
Inverse Operations

Opposite operations that "undo" each other.

Addition ↔ Subtraction; Multiplication ↔ Division
Isolate the Variable

The goal of solving: use inverse operations until the variable stands alone on one side.

\(3x = 15 \rightarrow x = 5\)
Like Terms

Terms with the exact same variable part - combine by adding/subtracting coefficients.

\(5x\) and \(-2x\)
One- & Two-Step Equations: The Core Pattern
Every equation-solving move follows the same rule: whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other. To undo an operation, apply its inverse to both sides.
Inverse Operations Table
If the equation shows...InverseAction on BOTH sides
Addition (\(+5\))↔Subtract 5
Subtraction (\(-8\))↔Add 8
Multiplication (\(3x\))↔Divide by 3
Division (\(\frac{x}{4}\))↔Multiply by 4
Worked Example: One Step

\(x + 9 = 15\). Subtract 9 from both sides: \(x = 6\).

Worked Example: Two Steps

\(3x + 4 = 19\). This equation shows multiplication AND addition happening to \(x\) - undo them in reverse order (addition/subtraction first, then multiplication/division).

Step 1 - Subtract 4 from both sides: \(3x = 15\)

Step 2 - Divide both sides by 3: \(x = \mathbf{5}\)

Multi-Step Equations: Simplify First
When an equation has parentheses or terms to combine, that's not a solving step yet - it's a simplifying step. Always simplify each side completely first, and only then start isolating the variable.
Worked Example: Distribute, Combine, Then Isolate

Solve \(3(x + 2) = 21\).

Step 1 - Distribute: \(3x + 6 = 21\)

Step 2 - Subtract 6 from both sides: \(3x = 15\)

Step 3 - Divide both sides by 3: \(x = \mathbf{5}\)

Worked Example: Variable on Both Sides

Solve \(5x + 3 = 2x + 12\). First collect the variable terms onto one side.

Step 1 - Subtract \(2x\) from both sides: \(3x + 3 = 12\)

Step 2 - Subtract 3 from both sides: \(3x = 9\)

Step 3 - Divide both sides by 3: \(x = \mathbf{3}\)

From Numbers to Letters: What Actually Changes
Here's the preview for Literal Equations: every step you just reviewed works identically when some of the "numbers" in the equation are replaced with other letters. You do the exact same inverse operations, in the exact same order, on both sides. The only difference is that your final answer is an expression instead of a single number.

Solving With Numbers (what you know)

Solve for \(x\): \(ax = b\)... but with real numbers:

\(5x = 20\)

Divide both sides by 5: \(x = \mathbf{4}\)

Solving With a Letter Instead (what's coming)

Solve for \(x\): \(ax = b\)

The "5" is now the letter \(a\) - treat it exactly like a number.

Divide both sides by \(a\): \(x = \mathbf{\dfrac{b}{a}}\)

Same Process, Different-Looking Answer

Notice the process was identical in both columns above - "divide both sides by whatever is multiplying \(x\)." The only difference is that when it's a letter instead of a number, you can't simplify the division any further, so the letter stays in the answer. That's really all Literal Equations adds.
